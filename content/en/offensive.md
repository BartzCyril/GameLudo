In Ludo, the die decides a lot, but **the choice of which pawn to move makes the difference**. A good attacking strategy rests on two ideas: **anticipating your opponents' moves** so you capture at the right moment, and **moving several pawns forward** so you always have a good move to play.

## Anticipate your opponents' moves

Before rolling the die, look at the board:

- **where are the opponents' pawns compared to yours?** An opponent's pawn 1 to 6 squares in front of one of your pawns can be captured on the next roll, with the right number;
- **which opponents' pawns are dangerous for you?** The same distances apply the other way round: an opponent right behind you can capture you;
- **where will new pawns appear?** The squares after an opponent's starting square fill up as soon as they roll a 6.

By predicting their moves, you can adjust your strategy: set up a capture, block their progress or avoid their attacks.

## Pick the right captures

Capturing an opponent's pawn sends it back to its base: they will need another 6 and must start all over again. But not all captures are equal.

**A capture is excellent when:**

- the opponent's pawn was **far ahead**: they lose almost a full lap of the board;
- the opponent has **few pawns in play**: they may wait a long time for their next 6;
- your pawn is **not exposed** right after, or only to one opponent's pawn.

**Think twice when:**

- it puts your pawn **right in front of several opponents**;
- it means giving up on **getting a pawn to safety** in the home column.

## Keep your pawns moving together

Focusing all your efforts on a single pawn is tempting: it gets home fast. But it is risky:

- a lone pawn is an **easy target**, and losing it wipes out all your efforts;
- with only one pawn in play, you have **only one possible move** on each roll, even if it is a bad one;
- at the end of the game, the exact-roll rule can **block you** for several turns.

With **several pawns in play**, every roll gives you a choice: capture with one, escape with another, get home with a third. The more options you have, the more the luck of the die works in your favour.

## Make the most of your 6s

The 6 is the most valuable roll. At the start of the game, you must use it to bring a pawn out if you still have one in your base ([see the rules](@gameplay)). Once all your pawns are out, remember that a 6 lets you:

- **catch an opponent's pawn** exactly 6 squares ahead of you;
- **get a pawn out of the danger zone** in one go;
- **bring a pawn closer to home** for the final stretch.

## Attack without forgetting defence

Attack and defence go hand in hand. After every attacking move, ask yourself whether your pawn is safe. If not, the article on [defensive strategies](@defensive) will help you protect your pawns, and the one on [observation and adaptation strategies](@observation) will help you choose between risk and caution depending on the stage of the game.

## Key takeaways

- Look at the board before every roll.
- Go after the most advanced opponents' pawns first.
- Keep several pawns in play.
- After every attack, check that your pawn is not exposed.
