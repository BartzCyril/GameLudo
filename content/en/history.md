Ludo is **the heir of Pachisi, a race game from India** played on a cross-shaped board. When it reached Europe in the 19th century, it was simplified and renamed: **Ludo** in England, **les petits chevaux** in France. Here is the story of a game several centuries old.

## Pachisi, the Indian ancestor

Long before Ludo, people in India played **Pachisi**. Its name comes from the Hindi word *paccīs*, which means **"twenty-five"**: it is the highest score you can get when throwing **cowrie shells**, small shells used instead of dice. The number of shells that land with their opening facing up tells you how far to move.

The exact origins of the game are still debated. Cross-shaped boards have been known since ancient times on the Indian subcontinent, and a board identical to the Pachisi board was found in the Ellora caves, in India. The game is often said to be more than 1,500 years old.

## The game of the Mughal emperors

Pachisi was also a court game. According to accounts, the Mughal emperor **Akbar** played it on **large boards laid out on the floor** of his palaces, with people as pawns. Courts of this kind were reportedly built in Agra and Allahabad.

Over the centuries, the game spread to many regions, taking on different names and variants. In India, it is still played in many forms, with boards often embroidered on cloth and rules specific to each region.

## Arrival in Europe and the birth of Ludo

The game reached Europe in the **19th century**, especially England, through the British presence in India. The English simplified it: the cowrie shells were replaced by **a six-sided die**, and you needed a 6 to bring a pawn out.

In **1896**, this version was **patented in England by Alfred Collier under the name Ludo**, from the Latin *ludo*, "I play". Easy to learn and very competitive, Ludo quickly won over families.

## A family of games all over the world

From the same principle, each country developed its own version:

| Country | Name of the game |
| --- | --- |
| India | Pachisi (and Chaupar, a close variant) |
| United Kingdom | Ludo |
| United States | Parcheesi |
| Spain | Parchís |
| Germany | Mensch ärgere dich nicht |
| France | Le jeu des petits chevaux |

All these variants share the essentials: a track, pawns to bring home and opponents you can send back to their base.

## Les petits chevaux, the French version

In France, the game is called **le jeu des petits chevaux**, "the little horses game", because its pawns are shaped like **horses** that leave their **stable** and must return to it. The board keeps the cross shape: each player has **four pawns** to move from the start to the finish according to the roll of the die.

**The goal is to be the first player to bring all their pawns home.** The full rules are explained in our articles on [starting a game](@start), [playing a game](@gameplay) and [the end of the game](@end).

## A classic gone digital

The game has never stopped evolving. After wooden and cardboard boards, there are now online versions that let you play alone against the computer, with friends or against players from all over the world.
